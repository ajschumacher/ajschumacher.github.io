🌍 Thirteen million

Here is the biggest number in this course so far. Every year, about 13 million babies are born preterm — more than one in every ten babies on Earth. Line them up and it's a city bigger than New York, born every year, too soon. Most of them, with warmth, feeding help, and someone watching their breathing, would grow up fine. Yet roughly 900,000 children a year die from the complications of being born early, which makes prematurity the number-one cause of death in children under five, anywhere in the world.

A sociologist's first question is: which babies? Not one in ten everywhere. The rate is about 6% in some countries and about 16% in others — Bangladesh, Malawi, and Pakistan have some of the highest — and more than six in ten of all preterm births happen in just two regions, sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. But the sharpest fact isn't about where babies are born early. It's about what happens next. In the world's richest countries, a baby born at 30 weeks almost always survives. In the poorest, about half of babies born at or before 32 weeks die — not of anything exotic, but for lack of the ordinary things you learned in NICU Tutor: warmth, help with feeding, treatment for infections and breathing trouble. The same baby, born in two different places, gets two different lives.

📊 Why sociologists count “per thousand”

To compare places, you can't just count. Country A has 200,000 babies die and Country B has 20,000 — but if A has ten times as many births, they're doing equally well. So sociologists (and doctors, and everyone who compares groups) use rates: how many per hundred, or per thousand. You met “per” in Calculus — it means divide. A preterm rate of 10% means 10 preterm babies for every 100 born. An infant death rate of 5.6 per 1,000 means that out of every thousand babies born, about five or six die before their first birthday. Turn counts into rates, and suddenly a village and a nation can be compared on the same line.

Now use the tool on the United States, one of the richest countries on Earth. The overall infant death rate is about 5.6 per 1,000 — better than most of the world, worse than most rich countries. But split it by group and the line breaks in two. For white babies, about 4.5 per thousand. For Black babies, about 10.9 — more than twice as many. Black babies are also born preterm far more often: about 14.6% versus about 9.4%. And a baby born in a rural county may be hours from a hospital that delivers babies at all: more than two million American women of childbearing age live in what the March of Dimes calls a maternity care desert — a county with no hospital or clinic providing birth care. Remember regionalization from last week, the plan to get the sickest babies to a Level III or IV unit? It only works if the unit is reachable. For a lot of families, it's a three-hour drive.

🗺️ It isn't the babies

So why? The tempting answer is that some babies are just born weaker. The evidence says no. When researchers look closely, the gaps track conditions of life, not biology: whether the mother had a doctor to see during pregnancy; whether she had enough to eat and a safe place to live; how far the hospital was; how much stress she carried, month after month, from money, from work, from being treated badly; whether anyone listened when she said something felt wrong. Sociologists call these the social determinants of health — the ordinary circumstances that decide, before a single machine is switched on, who gets sick and who gets care. Doctors have a blunter phrase: your ZIP code predicts your health better than your genetic code.

Here is the proof that it's the circumstances and not the babies: change the circumstances and the numbers move. When a poor country trains nurses in kangaroo care and hands out plastic wraps to keep newborns warm, its preterm deaths fall — the Bogotá idea from Psychology, now recommended by the World Health Organization for every small baby on Earth, saving an estimated hundred thousand lives a year at almost no cost. When a rural region opens a clinic or sends a nurse to visit pregnant mothers at home, its babies arrive later and bigger. When a hospital simply decides to measure its gap by race and go after it — more visits, more listening, more translators, earlier care — the gap narrows. None of that changes anyone's DNA. It changes who gets care.

🔬 Fieldwork: how far is your NICU?

You need: a grown-up, a map app, and ten minutes.

Sociologists do fieldwork. Yours: with a grown-up, find the nearest hospital to your home that has a NICU (search “Level III NICU near me” — a hospital's website usually says its level). How far is it, and how long is the drive? Now find the nearest hospital that delivers babies at all. Then imagine your family lived two hours from either one. What would change about a preterm birth? Write your distances on the homework — and if you have a friend or cousin in a different town, ask them the same question and compare.

⏪ Flashback: the warming machine that's always available

NICU Tutor Lesson 1 ended with this: where incubators are scarce, cold still kills newborns, so doctors teach kangaroo care everywhere, “because a parent's chest is a warming machine that's always available.” Today you learned the sociology behind that sentence: the machine isn't the problem. Getting care to the baby is.

🏠 Real talk: the doctor who turned her house into a hospital

In 1931, a young Black doctor in Philadelphia named Virginia M. Alexander looked at the numbers for her own neighborhood and did what a sociologist would do next: she acted on them. Black families in North Philadelphia were being turned away from hospitals or given the worst beds; mothers gave birth at home without help; babies died at rates that the city's white neighborhoods never saw. So she opened a clinic in her own house on North 15th Street and called it the Aspiranto Health Home: a few beds upstairs where mothers could deliver safely, care for their newborns, and pay whatever they could, which was often nothing. She ran it for years, then went to Yale to study public health so she could prove with statistics what she'd seen with her eyes — and wrote reports on the health gap that the city couldn't ignore. She was a Quaker, a friend of the civil-rights lawyer Pauli Murray, and she died in 1949 at only 49, of lupus, still working.

The honest part is that the gaps she measured are still there, ninety years later, in the table above. That's not a reason to shrug. It's the reason this course exists: a NICU can be the best in the world and still only save the babies who reach it. Some of you will run NICUs. Some of you will decide where to build them, who to send a nurse to, and which numbers to measure. Alexander's lesson is that the second job matters as much as the first — and that it starts with counting, honestly, who isn't getting care.

📖 Words to know
Rate
A count divided by the size of the group — per 100 or per 1,000 — so different groups can be compared.
Infant mortality
Babies who die before their first birthday, usually given per 1,000 births.
Disparity
A gap between groups that shouldn't be there — in care, in survival, in health.
Social determinants of health
The conditions of life — money, housing, food, stress, distance, respect — that shape who gets sick and who gets care.
Maternity care desert
A county with no hospital or clinic that delivers babies.
Public health
Caring for the health of whole groups and places, not one patient at a time.
Fieldwork
Going and looking: the sociologist's way of collecting data.
