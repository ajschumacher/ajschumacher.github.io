🌈 White light is a mixture

Sunlight looks white, or maybe faintly yellow. In 1666, a young Isaac Newton — yes, the Calculus one — darkened his room, let a thin beam of sunlight through a hole in the shutter, and put a glass prism in its path. The beam came out the other side as a fan of color: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet. White light wasn't one thing. It was all the colors travelling together, and the glass had bent each color by a slightly different amount and spread them apart. A rainbow is the same trick done by raindrops.

What is the difference between red and blue? Light travels as waves, and the colors are different wavelengths — different spacing between the ripples. Red waves are long and lazy; violet waves are short and tight. Every color in the rainbow sits in order of wavelength, red to violet. And the rainbow doesn't stop where your eyes do. Past red, with even longer waves, is infrared — invisible, but you can feel it as warmth on your skin, and your TV remote talks in it. Past violet, with even shorter waves, is ultraviolet — invisible to us, visible to bees, and the part of sunlight that burns your skin.

Light is also absurdly fast: about 300,000 kilometers every second. Sunlight takes eight minutes to reach you. Light from the monitor above a NICU bed reaches the nurse's eyes in about a billionth of a second — which is why nobody had to invent a faster way to look at things.

🎨 Why things have color

Here's a question that sounds silly and isn't: why is a tomato red? White light — all the colors — lands on the tomato. The tomato's skin absorbs most of the colors, soaking them up like a sponge, and bounces back the red. The red is what reaches your eye. A leaf absorbs red and blue and bounces green. White paper bounces everything; a black shirt absorbs everything (which is why it gets hot in the sun — absorbed light turns into warmth). The color of a thing is the light it refuses.

Now apply that to blood. Blood is red because hemoglobin absorbs blue and green and bounces red. But here's the detail that a whole machine is built on: hemoglobin changes color depending on whether it's carrying oxygen. Loaded with oxygen, it's bright cherry red — it lets red light pass through easily. Empty, it's dark, purplish red — it absorbs more red light. That's why the blood in your veins, on its way back to the heart with its oxygen delivered, looks darker than blood from a cut.

🔬 Home lab: your finger is a pulse oximeter

You need: a small flashlight (a phone's works) and a dark room.

Press the flashlight against the pad of your fingertip so the light shines into your finger. Look at the far side: your whole fingertip glows orange-red. White light went in; only red came out. The blood and flesh absorbed the blue and green on the way through and let the red pass — exactly the trick the pulse oximeter uses. Bonus: hold a strip of red cellophane and then blue cellophane in front of the flashlight and see which color makes it through your finger. The homework has a place to record it.

🔴 The pulse oximeter, explained at last

In NICU Tutor you learned that the pulse oximeter shines two colors through a baby's foot and works out the oxygen saturation. Now you can see how. The band holds two tiny lights on one side and a detector on the other. One light is red; the other is infrared — the invisible color past red. Both shine through the foot, and the detector measures how much of each gets through.

  • Oxygen-loaded hemoglobin lets red through easily but absorbs a good deal of infrared.
  • Empty hemoglobin does the opposite: it absorbs more red and lets more infrared through.

So the machine compares: how much red made it through, versus how much infrared? That ratio tells it the mix of loaded to empty hemoglobin — the percentage you know as SpO₂. Two colors, one division problem, and a number every three seconds.

But there was a catch that stumped engineers for decades. Light going through a foot doesn't just pass through blood. It passes through skin, bone, fat, and the blood sitting in the veins — and all of that absorbs light too, differently in every baby. How do you measure only the fresh, oxygen-rich blood in the arteries? In 1972, a Japanese engineer named Takuo Aoyagi noticed something in his data that everyone else had treated as annoying noise: the light signal pulsed. With every heartbeat a little extra blood surged into the foot, and the light dimmed a tiny bit, then brightened. Skin and bone don't pulse; only arterial blood does. So Aoyagi threw away the steady part of the signal and kept only the pulsing part — and the pulsing part was pure artery. He'd invented the pulse oximeter, and the reason it has “pulse” in its name is that a heartbeat is the only thing in a foot that changes fast.

⏪ Flashback: “machines report; humans judge”

Now you can explain the pulse ox's famous weakness from NICU Tutor Lesson 2. If the band slips and the light shines past the foot onto the mattress — or if bright room light leaks into the detector — the machine is measuring a light signal that has nothing to do with blood, and it reports a number anyway. A kicking baby makes the signal pulse for reasons that aren't heartbeats. The number is physics; whether it means anything is a nurse's judgment.

🔵 The lamp that cures a disease

The other light machine is the bili light. You know the disease: jaundice, the yellow color of a newborn whose blood is loaded with bilirubin, the leftover from recycling red blood cells, piling up faster than a brand-new liver can clear it. Bilirubin is a yellow molecule — which, after this morning, tells you something: it absorbs blue light and bounces yellow. That absorbed blue light is the cure.

When a bilirubin molecule absorbs blue light (the best wavelength is around 460 nanometers, a deep sky blue), the energy twists the molecule into a slightly different shape. The twisted shape dissolves in water, and the body can flush it out in urine and bile without the liver's help. The liver enzyme the baby is still building? Not needed. The light does its job for it, right through the skin. That's why babies under bili lights wear nothing but a diaper (more skin, more cure) and little eye shields (a precaution — a retina that's still growing doesn't need hours of bright blue light).

And here's a pattern you'll see all semester. The discovery wasn't made by a physicist. In 1956, at a hospital in Rochford, England, a nurse named Sister Jean Ward believed fresh air was good for babies and wheeled the premature ones into the sunny courtyard. One day a doctor noticed something odd about a jaundiced baby: the skin was pale where the sun had reached it and still yellow in the shape of the diaper and the sheet's fold. Ward had already noticed. Around the same time, a lab technician found that a tube of blood left in the sunlight measured less bilirubin than it should. Two clues, and Dr. Richard Cremer put them together, built a lamp, and by 1958 had published the first light treatment for jaundice. Today it's used on millions of babies a year, and the machine in the corner of every NICU began with a nurse who noticed a diaper-shaped shadow.

👁️ Real talk: the light that didn't cause blindness

Back in NICU Tutor, you learned about retinopathy of prematurity — ROP, the eye disease of preemies, caused mostly by too much oxygen. For decades, some doctors worried about something else: the bright lights of the NICU shining into eyes that were still under construction. It was a reasonable fear. Light and a growing retina; who could say? So in the 1990s researchers ran a proper test on hundreds of tiny preemies: half wore light-blocking goggles from birth, half didn't, and everyone's eyes were checked. Result: no difference. The lights weren't the culprit. The oxygen was. The goggles came off, and the worry went where worries should go when the evidence says so — away.

Why does that matter for physics? Because light is a physical thing with a measurable dose, like oxygen and pressure. Bili-light blue at the right wavelength cures; the same lamp's brightness in a growing eye is a question you answer with a test, not a guess. Statistics will show you next week how such a test is built. Physics gives you the wavelength; only measuring gives you the answer.

📖 Words to know
Spectrum
The rainbow of colors that white light spreads into.
Wavelength
The spacing between a light wave's ripples. Long = red; short = violet.
Infrared
Invisible light with longer waves than red. Felt as warmth; used by the pulse ox.
Ultraviolet
Invisible light with shorter waves than violet. The sunburn part of sunlight.
Absorb
To soak up light. A thing's color is the light it doesn't absorb.
Prism
A wedge of glass that bends each color differently and spreads them apart.
Phototherapy
Treating with light. The bili light is phototherapy for jaundice.
