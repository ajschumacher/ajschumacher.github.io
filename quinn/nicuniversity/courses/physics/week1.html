Pressure

Right now, the air is pushing on every square inch of you with about fifteen pounds of force — and you don't feel a thing. Physics is the science of pushes like that, and the NICU runs on them: in blood vessels, in breathing machines, in the air itself.

👍 What pressure is

Press your thumb against a corkboard as hard as you like — nothing happens. Now put a thumbtack between your thumb and the board and press the same way: the tack sinks right in. Same push, completely different result. The difference is pressure.

Pressure is a push spread over an area. Your thumb spreads its push over a big pad of skin. The tack's point takes that same push and delivers it to a spot smaller than a grain of sand — so the push on each tiny bit of cork is enormous. In numbers: pressure = push ÷ area. Same push, one-fourth the area, four times the pressure. That's the whole idea, and it explains a surprising amount of the world: why snowshoes keep you from sinking (big area, low pressure), why knives are sharp (tiny area, high pressure), and why a NICU nurse presses on a baby's fingernail with a fingertip instead of a fingernail when checking circulation.

📝
Write this down · big idea 1

Pressure = push ÷ area. Same push on a smaller area = bigger pressure (that's why sharp things go in).

🌍 The air is pushing on you right now

Air doesn't feel like anything, but it has weight — and there are about a hundred kilometers of it stacked above your head. All of that weight presses down on you, on the table, on everything: the pressure of the atmosphere. You don't notice it because the air and fluids inside your body push back out exactly as hard. (Inside you, it's a tie.)

In 1643 an Italian scientist named Evangelista Torricelli proved this with a beautiful trick. He filled a glass tube with liquid mercury — a silvery metal that's liquid at room temperature and very, very heavy — closed the top, and stood it upside down in a dish of mercury. Some mercury ran out of the tube, but not all of it: a column about 760 millimeters tall stayed standing, held up by nothing but the weight of the air pressing down on the dish. The air was strong enough to hold up 760 mm of the heaviest liquid he had. He'd built the first barometer, and invented a way to measure pressure: millimeters of mercury, written mmHg (Hg is mercury's chemical symbol — from the Latin hydrargyrum, “liquid silver.” Chemistry sneaks in everywhere).

Five years later, Blaise Pascal asked his brother-in-law to carry a Torricelli tube up a mountain in France. Near the top, the mercury column was several centimeters shorter — less air above you, less push. Pascal was right: the air is an ocean, and we live at the bottom of it. The modern unit of pressure is named the pascal in his honor.

And here is why this matters in a NICU: blood pressure is measured in mmHg because the first blood pressure meters (1896) were literally a cuff connected to a mercury column. A baby's blood pressure of “60” means the heart is pushing hard enough to lift a column of mercury 60 mm high. Now you know what the number is.

📝
Write this down · big idea 2

Air has weight. At sea level it pushes hard enough to hold up 760 mm of mercury — so we measure pressure (including blood pressure) in mmHg.

🏥 Pressure in the NICU

Walk down a NICU and nearly every machine is a pressure machine:

  • The blood pressure cuff inflates until its pressure is greater than the artery's, squeezing it shut — then lets go slowly and notices the exact moment blood starts pushing through again. That moment is the top number. (Flashback: NICU Tutor Lesson 4.)
  • CPAP holds a preemie's lungs open with a gentle, constant pressure — so gentle that mmHg is too big a unit for it. Breathing support is measured in centimeters of water, cmH₂O: how high the push could lift a column of water instead of mercury. Typical CPAP is about 5 to 8 cmH₂O.
  • Bubble CPAP makes the physics visible: the breathing-out tube ends under water in a bottle. Push the tube 5 cm deep and the baby breathes out against exactly 5 cmH₂O of pressure — the bubbles are the proof. Set the pressure by moving a tube up and down in water. Torricelli would have loved it.
  • The ventilator pushes each breath in with a measured pressure — often 15 to 25 cmH₂O at the peak — then lets the chest fall.

Two units, so which is bigger? Mercury is about 13.6 times heavier than water, so lifting a millimeter of mercury takes as much push as lifting 13.6 millimeters — 1.36 centimeters — of water. That means 1 mmHg ≈ 1.36 cmH₂O: the mercury unit is the bigger push, even though millimeters are smaller than centimeters. A CPAP of 6 cmH₂O is only about 4 mmHg — a whisper compared with a blood pressure of 60.

🧪 Try it yourself

Fill a glass with water, put a straw in, and put your finger tightly over the top of the straw. Lift the straw out: the water stays inside! Nothing is holding it up but the air pressing on the open bottom end — a tiny Torricelli tube. Lift your finger and the air can push from the top too; the tie is broken and the water falls.

💔 Real talk: pressure heals and pressure hurts

In the 1960s, doctors began putting the smallest babies on ventilators, and babies who would certainly have died started surviving. But many survivors came home with scarred, stiff lungs — a new disease, named bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) in 1967, caused partly by the machines themselves: too much pressure, for too long, stretching alveoli that were never built to take it. Sometimes an over-stretched air sac simply pops, and air leaks into the chest and squashes the lung — an air leak, which can turn deadly in minutes and has to be drained with a needle or tube.

Physics gave doctors the way out too. In 1971, George Gregory in San Francisco tried something gentler on preemies with collapsing lungs: not big breaths, just a steady, low pressure to keep the alveoli from closing — the first CPAP. Survival jumped. Today's rule, “the smallest pressure that works,” comes straight out of that history. The people who set those dials know that the same push that keeps a lung open can also tear it — and they measure every centimeter of water.

📖 Words to know
Pressure
A push spread over an area. Push ÷ area.
Atmosphere
The ocean of air we live at the bottom of. Its weight presses on everything.
Barometer
An instrument that measures air pressure — Torricelli's tube was the first.
mmHg
Millimeters of mercury: how high the push could lift mercury. Blood pressure's unit.
cmH₂O
Centimeters of water: how high the push could lift water. CPAP and ventilator unit.
Air leak
A popped air sac leaking air into the chest — a danger of too much pressure.
