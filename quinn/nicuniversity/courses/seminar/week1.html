🏛️ What a university is

A university is a place where people go to learn hard things deeply and, just as importantly, where people discover new things. Your teachers are called professors, and most of them do research when they aren't teaching — finding out things nobody knew before. The first universities were started almost a thousand years ago (Bologna, in Italy, in 1088), and the first thing students there argued about was… medicine.

A student earning a first degree is an undergraduate. That usually takes four years. Someone who wants to be a doctor spends those years taking science courses — chemistry, biology, physics, statistics, and more — to get ready for medical school, which comes after. Those preparation courses are called pre-med. So “pre-med undergraduate” means: a university student getting ready for medical school. That's you, starting today.

Here's the honest map of the road to becoming a neonatologist — a doctor for newborns — in the United States: about four years of university, four years of medical school, three years training in children's medicine (called residency), and three more years training in newborn intensive care (a fellowship). Fourteen years after high school. It's a long road. Nobody walks it all at once; you walk it one class at a time. Today is a class.

📅 How classes work here

Look at your timetable. Every box is a class. The smart way through is in order, like a real week — but any class is open whenever you want it. There are three kinds:

  • Lectures (like this one) — the professor teaches something new. About 20 minutes, with an 8-question quiz at the end. Score 6 or better and you earn the course's badge for the week.

✍️ The one rule of note-taking

In NICU Tutor, you took notes — and you noticed something: writing down everything is exhausting, and it doesn't actually help you remember. Meanwhile writing down almost nothing leaves you with… almost nothing. Real university students struggle with exactly this. Here's the rule that fixes it:

Notes are for future-you. Write the 3 biggest ideas, in your own words.

Why three? Because future-you — the you taking Friday's exam — needs to remember the ideas, not the sentences. Why your own words? Because you can copy a sentence without understanding it, but you cannot rewrite an idea in your own words without understanding it first. The rewriting is the learning. That's the secret.

Here's the difference. Suppose the professor says: “A premature baby cannot keep itself warm because its skin is thin, it has almost no fat for insulation, it cannot shiver, and it has very little brown fat. The isolette warms the air automatically using a skin probe.”

❌ Copying everything✅ Three big ideas, my words
A premature baby cannot keep itself warm because its skin is thin, it has almost no fat for insulation, it cannot shiver, and it has very little brown fat. The isolette warms the air automatically using a skin probe. • Preemies can't stay warm on their own (thin skin, no fat, no shivering)
• Isolette = warm box that fixes it
• It uses a skin probe to adjust itself

The right column is shorter, and it's better — because whoever wrote it had to understand the paragraph to squeeze it down. And it's faster, so you can keep listening instead of scribbling.

To make this easy, every lecture comes with a one-page Class Notes sheet with three boxes for big ideas, two rows for new words (in your words), a box to draw something, and a spot for one question you still have. And in Week 1, the lecture tells you the big ideas — look for the dashed “Write this down” boxes. Next week, the boxes will ask you to find the big idea yourself. By Week 3 you won't need them.

🧠 How to take a quiz (and how not to)

You've taken nine NICU Tutor quizzes, so you know the shape. Here's the pro version:

  1. Read the question twice. Half of all wrong answers come from answering a question that wasn't asked.
  2. Read every choice before picking. Even if the first one looks right.
  3. Cross out the wrong ones. Usually you can eliminate two. Now it's a fair fight between the last two.
  4. Don't guess from looks. Some students learn to pick the longest answer, or the one in the middle. It won't work here: NICUniversity puts the right answer in a random position, and the wrong answers are written to sound just as reasonable. You actually have to know it. That's a compliment.
  5. Missing one is fine. Every wrong answer here comes with an explanation — that's where learning happens. You can retake any quiz as many times as you like.

Homework is different from a quiz: it isn't multiple choice. You'll write short answers, label drawings, fill in tables, and do a little math. The answer key has sample answers, and your professor grades it — if your answer says the same thing in different words, it counts.

One more real-university thing: office hours. That's when a professor sits in their office and students drop in to ask anything. Your professor's office hours are… whenever you ask. Use them. Asking questions isn't cheating; it's the whole point of having a professor.

🎓 Real talk: the student nobody wanted

In the 1840s, no medical school in the United States would admit a woman. Elizabeth Blackwell applied anyway — and was rejected by more than two dozen schools. Then Geneva Medical College in New York put the question to its 150 male students, who voted to let her in as a joke. She showed up. Professors tried to keep her out of anatomy lessons; townspeople crossed the street to avoid her. In 1849 she graduated first in her class — the first woman in America to earn a medical degree — and went on to found a hospital and a medical college for women.

Why tell you this on day one? Because being a student is not the same as being welcomed. Sometimes you'll be the youngest, or the only one who cares this much, or the one people didn't expect. Blackwell's answer was to do the work and let the transcript speak. Yours can be too.
