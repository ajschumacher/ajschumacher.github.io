🎯 Big idea or detail?

A lecture has around a hundred sentences. Three or four of them are load-bearing — take one away and the whole thing falls down. The rest are supports: examples, numbers, stories, jokes, the professor clearing their throat. Supports are wonderful; they're how the big ideas get into your head. But they're not what future-you needs to write down, because future-you can rebuild a support from an idea, and can't rebuild an idea from a support.

So how do you tell them apart while the professor is still talking? Three tests:

  1. The “so what?” test. Ask what the sentence makes true. “Air is about 78% nitrogen” — so what? So most of what you breathe, your body ignores. That “so what” is the idea; 78% is the support.
  2. The “forget it” test. Imagine you forgot this sentence. Would the rest of the lecture still make sense? If yes, it's a detail. If everything after it stops making sense, it's an idea. Forget “Torricelli did it in 1643” and you're fine. Forget “air has weight and pushes on everything” and nothing about blood pressure makes sense.
  3. The “repeat” test. Ideas get said more than once. They show up in the heading, then in a sentence, then in the summary. The professor says “here's the thing” or “the point is” before them. Details are said once and let go.

Try it on this sentence from last week's Chemistry: “Most of every breath is nitrogen, and your body does nothing with it — in and right back out, like a passenger who never gets off the bus.” The bus is a support (a lovely one). The idea is: most of the air is nitrogen, and the body doesn't use it. Seven words. Future-you can rebuild the bus.

🔍 Where ideas hide

Ideas have favorite hiding places, and once you know them you'll find ideas faster than the professor can say them:

  • Headings. A heading is the professor telling you what the next five minutes are for. “Pressure in the NICU” means the idea will be about pressure and the NICU. Read headings twice.
  • First and last sentences of a section. Professors (and writers) tend to state the idea, then explain it, then state it again.
  • Tables. Almost always details — unless the pattern in the table is the point. The pressure table last week had six numbers; the idea was “the atmosphere is huge, blood pressure is medium, CPAP is a whisper.”
  • Stories. The “Real talk” box has dates and names. The idea is the lesson of the story, not the date. Priestley's mouse: “oxygen is the part of air that living things need” — not “1774.”
  • Numbers. Keep only the ones a doctor would need at the bedside. 760 mmHg, 21%, 6 out of 8 — keep. The year, the name of Sørensen's brewery — let them go (they'll be there when you want them).

✅ The best way to check: don't reread — test

Suppose you've found what you think is the big idea. How do you know it stuck? Most students do the natural thing: read it again. And again. It feels like learning, because the words get familiar. But in 2006 two psychologists, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke, ran a simple experiment. Students read a science passage. Then half of them reread it, and half closed it and wrote down everything they could remember — a test, with no grades. A week later everyone was quizzed. The rereaders remembered about 40% of the ideas. The self-testers remembered about 60%. Same passage, same time spent. The difference was whether they had pulled the ideas out of their own heads.

Psychologists call that retrieval practice, and it's one of the most solid findings in all of learning science. Rereading puts information in front of your eyes. Retrieving it makes your brain go find it, and finding it is what builds the path. That's why this week's boxes make you write before you check. That's why the Friday review sheet asks you to say each idea before you read it. And that's why the homework today asks you to write a quiz question of your own: to write a good question, you have to know the idea well enough to know what a wrong answer would look like.

The very best version of retrieval practice is the oldest: teach it. Explain the idea to someone who wasn't there, without looking. Where you get stuck is exactly where your notes need work.

📓 Real talk: the notebook that drew a baby first

Five hundred years ago, an Italian painter filled thousands of pages with notes. About 7,000 of those pages survive, and they are the most famous notebooks in the world, because the painter was Leonardo da Vinci. They are not tidy. He wrote backwards, right to left, in mirror writing (he was left-handed and it kept the ink off his hand). He wrote himself to-do lists like “describe the tongue of the woodpecker.” He drew a machine, then wrote one line about how it worked. He asked questions in the margins and answered them pages later.

Around 1511, Leonardo studied the body of a mother who had died while pregnant, and drew what he found: a baby curled inside the womb, knees up, with its umbilical cord winding to the placenta. It is the first accurate drawing of a baby before birth in human history — five hundred years before ultrasound. Look at the page and you'll see the three things a great notebook does. He drew it. He asked: how does the baby breathe? (He guessed wrong — through the mother — and wrote the guess down anyway, so a later Leonardo could fix it.) And he wrote the idea, one line. Not every sentence he'd heard. The idea.

Your Class Notes sheet has a draw-it box, a question box, and three idea boxes. That's not an accident.

🧰 How this week's boxes work

  1. When you reach a purple “What's the big idea here?” box, stop and write your idea on the notes sheet first.
  2. Then click “Check your idea.” Compare. Same meaning in different words? Perfect. Yours is a detail (a number, a name, an example)? Cross it out and write the idea.
  3. Next week the boxes will only mark the spot: “Big idea here — write yours.” No check. By Week 5 there are no boxes at all, because you won't need them.

One more real-university skill: ask. Leonardo's margins were full of questions. If a section has no big idea you can find, that's not a failure — it's a question for office hours, and the question box on your sheet is where it goes.

📖 Words to know
Big idea
A load-bearing sentence. Forget it and the rest stops making sense.
Detail
A support: a number, a date, an example, a story. Rebuildable from the idea.
The “so what?” test
Ask what a sentence makes true. The answer is usually the idea.
Retrieval practice
Pulling an idea out of your own head — saying it, writing it, teaching it — instead of rereading it.
Mirror writing
Writing backwards so it reads normally in a mirror. Leonardo's habit.
Anatomy
The study of how the body is built. Leonardo did it by looking; you'll do it in Biology.
