How Fast Is the Baby Growing?

Every NICU baby is weighed every single night, to the gram. But the number on the scale isn't what the doctors are really after. They want to know something the scale can't say directly: how fast is it changing? Answering that question is what calculus is.

🧮 What calculus is

You know arithmetic: adding, subtracting, multiplying, dividing numbers that sit still. Calculus is the mathematics of numbers that move — things that change over time. It asks two big questions about anything that's changing:

  1. How fast is it changing? (How many grams per day is this baby gaining?)
  2. How much has piled up? (If the pump runs at 3 mL an hour, how much milk did the baby get today?)

Those two questions turn out to be two sides of one coin, and figuring that out was one of the great achievements of human thought. It happened in the late 1600s — twice. Isaac Newton in England and Gottfried Leibniz in Germany each invented calculus on their own, at nearly the same time. Then their friends spent thirty years accusing each other of stealing it. (Both were right that they'd invented it. Neither was gracious about it. Even geniuses can be silly.) Today, calculus is how we predict the weather, design bridges, fly rockets — and read a baby's growth chart.

⏱️ Rate of change: change ÷ time

Here are real-looking NICU numbers. A preemie weighs 900 grams on day 1 and 970 grams on day 8. How fast is she growing? Two steps:

Step 1 — How much did it change? Later weight minus earlier weight: 970 − 900 = 70 grams.

Step 2 — Over how much time? Day 8 minus day 1 = 7 days. Spread the change over the days: 70 ÷ 7 = 10 grams per day.

Rate of change = change ÷ time. This baby is growing at 10 grams per day.

The little word “per” is doing heavy lifting there. “Per day” means “for each day.” Whenever you see “per,” somebody divided: miles per hour, beats per minute, grams per day. Doctors go one step further and divide by the baby's size too, so babies of different sizes can be compared: a 1-kilogram baby gaining 20 grams a day is growing at 20 grams per kilogram per day — “20 g/kg/day.” A 2-kilogram baby would have to gain 40 grams a day to match that. (Remember the target from NICU Tutor: about 15 to 20 g/kg/day.)

Try one: a baby goes from 1,200 g on day 1 to 1,340 g on day 8. Change? 140 g. Time? 7 days. Rate? 140 ÷ 7 = 20 g/day. If that baby weighs about 1 kg, that's 20 g/kg/day — right on target. Rates are just careful subtraction and division. You can already do calculus's first job.

Rate of change = change ÷ time. (970 − 900) ÷ 7 days = 10 grams per day. “Per” means “divided by.”

📈 Seeing the rate: steepness

Numbers in a table are hard to feel. So doctors draw them: each night's weight becomes a dot, with the day along the bottom and the weight up the side. Connect the dots and you get a line — and the line's steepness shows the rate at a glance. A steep climb means fast growth. A gentle climb means slow growth. A flat line means no growth. And a line going downhill means the baby is losing weight — a negative rate of change.

That downhill stretch isn't always bad news. Almost every newborn loses weight in the first few days — mostly water — up to about 10% of birth weight, and then climbs back within a week or two. A doctor who only saw one dot might panic; a doctor who sees the whole line knows it's normal. In medicine, the shape of the line is called the baby's growth velocity — and “velocity” is exactly the calculus word for a rate of change.

🤯 The real secret of calculus (a preview)

Our baby gained 10 g/day on average over the week. But maybe she gained nothing for three days and then 23 grams a day for four — the average hides that. Zoom in: what's the rate on one day? On one hour? Right now, this instant? Calculus's big trick is finding the rate at a single moment by zooming in closer and closer. Mathematicians call that “right-now rate” the derivative. You just learned what it means. The fancy symbols come later — and now they'll never scare you.

📝
Write this down · big idea 3

On a graph, steepness = rate. Steep = fast, flat = none, downhill = losing (negative). Read the line, not one dot.

🧪 Try it yourself: your own growth rate

Ask a grown-up for two of your heights with dates — a doctor's visit last year and one this year, say. Change = this year's height − last year's. Time = the months between. Rate = change ÷ time, in centimeters per month. (Most 8-year-olds grow about half a centimeter a month.) Now you've computed your own growth velocity — the same math a neonatologist does at 3 a.m.

💔 Real talk: when the line goes flat

Growth isn't a bonus for a preemie — it's the treatment. The brain is growing fastest of all in those weeks, and babies whose weight line goes flat for too long have a higher chance of learning and development problems later. So a flat line is an alarm, even if the baby seems fine today. The team hunts for a reason (is the baby working too hard to breathe and burning the calories? fighting an infection? not absorbing feeds?) and adds fuel — more milk, or a powder called fortifier that packs extra calories and protein into each milliliter. Then they watch the line. Not the dot. The line.

That's why a NICU doctor can look at a weight chart the way you look at a face. Calculus is the grammar of that reading — and this week, you learned its first sentence.

📖 Words to know
Rate of change
How much something changes per unit of time. Change ÷ time.
Per
“For each.” It always means somebody divided.
Slope
The steepness of a line on a graph — which, on a weight graph, IS the rate.
Negative
Less than zero. A negative rate means going down.
Velocity
Speed with a direction. Doctors say “growth velocity.”
Derivative
The rate of change at a single instant — calculus's central idea. Preview only!
