❤️ A pump with four rooms

Your heart is a muscle — a fist-sized bag of muscle that squeezes about once a second, every second, for your whole life, and never takes a break. A newborn's is about the size of a walnut. It's hollow, and the hollow is divided into four rooms: two upstairs, two downstairs. The upstairs rooms are the atria (one is an atrium) — the receiving rooms, where blood arrives. The downstairs rooms are the ventricles — the pumping rooms, with thick muscular walls that squeeze blood out with force.

Here's the part most people never learn: the heart is really two pumps side by side, and they send blood to two different places.

  • The right side receives blood coming back from the body — dark red, low on oxygen, loaded with CO₂ — and pumps it to the lungs. In the lungs it does the gas exchange you learned last week: drops CO₂, grabs O₂, turns bright red.
  • The left side receives that bright-red blood back from the lungs and pumps it out to the whole body: brain, gut, kidneys, fingertips. Then it comes back to the right side, and around it goes.

Blood leaves the heart through arteries (thick, stretchy pipes under pressure — the blood-pressure pipes from NICU Tutor) and returns through veins (thinner, floppier, low pressure). Between the rooms are valves: one-way doors that slam shut so blood can't slosh backward. The “lub-dub” you hear with a stethoscope is doors closing — lub is the doors between the atria and ventricles; dub is the doors out to the arteries.

How fast does a walnut have to beat? A newborn's heart runs at about 120 to 160 beats a minute — you learned the range from the monitor lesson. Take 140. Multiply by 60 minutes: 8,400 beats an hour. Multiply by 24 hours: about 200,000 beats a day. An adult heart, at 70, manages about 100,000. A preemie's heart works twice as hard as yours, every day, from a body the weight of a bag of sugar.

🩸 What's in the red river

Blood looks like a plain red liquid. Spin a tube of it in a machine and it separates into layers, and you can see what it really is: a river of water carrying passengers.

PartHow muchWhat it does
PlasmaAbout 55%The water. It carries salt, sugar, proteins — and this morning's bicarbonate sponge.
Red blood cellsAbout 45%The oxygen carriers, stuffed with hemoglobin. The cells with no nucleus from last week.
White blood cellsLess than 1%The defenders. They hunt bacteria. (Biology will meet them properly in Week 4.)
PlateletsLess than 1%Tiny cell pieces that plug leaks — the start of a scab.

Now the number that changes how a NICU works. An adult has about 5 liters of blood — a big soda bottle and a half. A newborn has about 80 milliliters for every kilogram of body weight. So a 3-kilogram full-term baby has about 240 mL, a cup. And a 1-kilogram preemie has about 80 mL — a third of a cup. Its entire blood supply would fit in a shot glass with room to spare.

Think about what that means for a blood test. This morning you learned that a blood gas takes a few drops. But other tests take more — a milliliter or two — and a sick preemie may need many tests a day. Two milliliters from an 80-milliliter baby is 1 part in 40 of everything it has. Do that for a few weeks and you've drained a meaningful share of the baby's blood, one test at a time. Nobody bled the baby on purpose; the tests did. NICU teams have a name for the anemia this causes, count every drop they draw, and use the tiniest tubes made. And when the red cells run too low for the oxygen deliveries to keep up, they replace them: a transfusion of blood given by a stranger. (Sociology will get to those strangers in Week 4.)

🔀 The shortcut that has to close

Here is a puzzle. Before birth, a baby's lungs are folded up and full of fluid. They don't breathe; they can't. So where does the oxygen come from? From the mother, through the placenta and the umbilical cord. The cord has three pipes: two arteries carrying the baby's used blood out to the placenta, and one vein bringing fresh, oxygen-loaded blood back. (Those are the pipes the umbilical catheters from NICU Tutor slide into. The cord is the baby's original life support.)

But look back at the diagram. The right heart pumps blood to the lungs. If the lungs aren't doing anything, that's a wasted trip. So the fetal heart has shortcuts. One is a flap between the two atria, so blood can slip from the right side straight to the left. The other is a short pipe called the ductus arteriosus that connects the artery going to the lungs directly to the artery going to the body — a bypass around the lungs. Before birth, most of the blood takes the bypass.

Then the baby is born and takes its first breath. The lungs unfold, fill with air, and suddenly it's easy for blood to flow through them. Pressures shift, the flap between the atria snaps shut, and over the next day or three the ductus squeezes itself closed and turns into a strand of scar. The shortcut is gone; the two-loop system from the diagram takes over. It's one of the great engineering switches in nature, and it happens in every delivery room, usually without anyone noticing.

Usually. In a preemie, the ductus often doesn't get the message. It stays open — doctors say patent, which just means open — and the baby has a PDA: patent ductus arteriosus. Now blood pumped out to the body leaks back through the open pipe into the lungs. The lungs get flooded with extra blood, so breathing gets harder; the body — gut, kidneys, brain — gets less than its share. The team hears it as a whooshing murmur through the stethoscope, confirms it with an ultrasound of the heart (Physics will explain how sound makes pictures, in Week 3), and then decides: many small PDAs close by themselves; a big one may get a medicine that nudges it shut, or, rarely, a tiny plug threaded in through a vein.

🔬 Home lab: one-way doors in your own arm

You need: your arm, and a grown-up to watch. Nothing else.

Let one arm hang straight down for a minute until the veins on the back of your hand puff up (blood is pooling because it has to climb uphill to your heart). Now press one puffy vein with a fingertip and keep pressing. With a finger of the other hand, stroke along the vein away from the heart, toward your knuckles. The stretch behind your stroking finger stays flat — the blood you pushed can't fall back because a valve, a one-way door, is holding it. Lift the first finger and it refills instantly. Then raise your hand above your head and watch every vein empty: downhill to the heart, doors open. In 1628 an English doctor used exactly this trick to prove blood goes around in a loop. The homework has a place to record what you saw.

👑 Real talk: the king's doctor and the arithmetic that proved a loop

For 1,400 years, doctors believed something that sounds ridiculous now: that blood was made fresh in the liver, flowed out to the body, and was simply used up — soaked into the flesh like water into a sponge. Nobody had ever seen it come back. Then in 1628, William Harvey, physician to King Charles I of England, published a small book with a big argument, and the argument was mostly arithmetic. He measured how much blood a heart holds — about two ounces — and multiplied by beats per minute, then by minutes in an hour. The answer was hundreds of pounds of blood an hour, far more than a body weighs, far more than any liver could make. The only way the sums worked was if the same blood went around and around. Then he showed the one-way valves in the veins with the finger trick you just did. He was mocked for years. He was right.

Harvey's loop is why every NICU number you've learned makes sense together. A blood gas from the umbilical artery is a sample of blood on its way out to the body. A low blood pressure means the loop isn't pushing hard enough for the deliveries. A PDA is a hole in the loop. And a transfusion is topping up the river. The honest part: about 1 baby in 100 is born with something built differently in the heart, and a few of those need surgery in the first weeks of life. Most are found by a test you already know — a pulse oximeter on the right hand and a foot, at 24 hours old, looking for blood that isn't getting to the lungs. Tomorrow, Physics will show you what that little red light is actually doing.

📖 Words to know
Atrium / ventricle
Upstairs receiving room / downstairs pumping room. Two of each.
Artery / vein
Pipe carrying blood away from the heart / back to it.
Valve
A one-way door in the heart or a vein. “Lub-dub” is valves closing.
Plasma
The watery part of blood — about 55% — that carries everything else.
Platelets
Tiny cell fragments that plug leaks.
Circulation
Blood going around the loop: heart → lungs → heart → body → heart.
Ductus arteriosus
The fetal bypass pipe around the lungs. “Patent” (PDA) means it stayed open.
Transfusion
Giving a patient blood donated by someone else.
