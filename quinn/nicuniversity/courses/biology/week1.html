Cells: The Body's LEGO Bricks

Every baby in every NICU began as one single cell, smaller than the period at the end of this sentence. By the time you finish this class, you'll know what's inside that cell, how it became trillions, and why a 500-gram preemie's cells are working harder than yours ever will.

🧱 What a cell is

This morning you learned that everything is made of atoms. True — but a rock is made of atoms and a baby is made of atoms, and only one of them is alive. What's the difference? The baby's atoms are organized into cells: tiny, self-contained bags of chemistry that eat, grow, make copies of themselves, and respond to the world. A cell is the smallest thing that is definitely alive.

So here's the ladder of the world, smallest to biggest: atoms join into molecules; billions of molecules organize into a cell; cells team up into tissues (muscle, skin, nerve); tissues build organs (heart, lungs, brain); organs form systems; and all the systems together make an organism — a whole living thing. Chemistry lives on the bottom two rungs. Biology starts at the cell.

How many cells are you? Roughly 30 trillion — 30,000,000,000,000. A newborn has far fewer, and a 500-gram micro-preemie fewer still, but their number is climbing every second. Keep that in mind; it explains a lot about the NICU.

🔍 Inside a cell

A cell is not a blob. It's more like a tiny city with walls, a library, power plants, and streets. The main parts:

  • Membrane — the outer skin. It's not a solid wall: it's a gatekeeper that lets oxygen and food in, and waste out.
  • Cytoplasm — the jelly-like liquid filling the cell, where most chemistry happens.
  • Nucleus — the library. It holds the cell's complete instruction book, written in a molecule called DNA. Every cell in your body has a copy of the whole book. (Genetics 101 opens that book on Wednesday.)
  • Mitochondria — the power plants. And here is where this morning's chemistry pays off: mitochondria take the sugar (C₆H₁₂O₆) that blood delivers and the oxygen (O₂) that hemoglobin drops off, and they burn them together — a slow, controlled burn — to release energy. The exhaust from that burn is carbon dioxide and water. Which is exactly what you breathe out.

Think about that chain. Oxygen enters a baby's lungs, hemoglobin carries it to a cell, the membrane lets it in, a mitochondrion burns it with sugar, the CO₂ exhaust rides the blood back to the lungs and leaves on the next breath. The whole NICU — the oxygen blender, the pulse oximeter, the sugar drip — exists to keep that chain running in trillions of cells at once.

📝
Write this down · big idea 2

✂️ One cell becomes trillions

How does a body get from one cell to 30 trillion? Not by making cells one at a time. A cell grows, copies its instruction book, and then divides into two complete cells. Then both of those divide. Then all four. This is cell division, and the important thing about it is that it doubles:

Ten rounds and you've passed a thousand. Twenty rounds, a million. Around forty-some rounds gets you into the trillions — enough for a whole person. Doubling is sneaky like that: it starts slow and then explodes. (Calculus 101 tomorrow is going to have a lot to say about things that grow.)

Now here's the NICU connection. A baby born at 26 weeks is supposed to be inside its mother for 14 more weeks of furious cell division. Instead it has to do that growing in an isolette — and dividing cells are hungry: they need sugar and oxygen for energy, and protein and minerals to build new parts. Gram for gram, a preemie's cells are working far harder than yours. That's why the feeding lesson in NICU Tutor was all about calories and grams: growth is cells dividing, and cells dividing costs fuel.

🧪 Try it yourself: doubling with paper

Fold a sheet of paper in half. Now it's 2 layers. Fold again: 4. Again: 8. Keep going and count the layers each time (predict before you count!). Most people can't get past 7 folds — 128 layers — because doubling makes the stack thick so fast. If you could fold it 42 times, the stack would reach the Moon. That's what your cells did on the way to becoming you.

🩸 Not all cells are alike

Every cell carries the same instruction book, but different cells read different chapters — so a nerve cell, a skin cell, and a blood cell look and act completely differently. Two kinds you already know from NICU Tutor:

  • Red blood cells are the strangest cells you own. As they mature they throw out their nucleus — no library! — to make room for cargo: each one is a bag stuffed with about 270 million hemoglobin molecules. But a cell with no instruction book can't repair itself, so red blood cells wear out after about four months and get recycled. And the yellow leftover from that recycling is… bilirubin. The reason newborns turn yellow is a cell biology story.
  • Skin cells stack in layers to build a wall. An adult's wall is dozens of layers thick and tough. A very premature baby's skin is only a few cell layers deep — so thin you can see through it — which is the cell-level reason a preemie leaks heat and water, and why the isolette has to be so warm and humid.
⏪ Flashback: two mysteries, now solved at the cell level

NICU Tutor Lesson 7 told you bilirubin is leftover from recycling red blood cells — now you know why they need recycling (no nucleus, no repairs). And Lesson 1 said preemie skin is thin — now you know what “thin” means: fewer layers of cells.

🔬 Real talk: the cloth merchant who saw the invisible

Nobody saw a cell until the 1660s, because nobody had a lens good enough. In 1665, an Englishman named Robert Hooke looked at a thin slice of cork through his microscope and saw rows of tiny empty boxes. They reminded him of the little bare rooms where monks slept — called cells — so that's what he named them. The name stuck for all of biology.

A few years later, a Dutch cloth merchant with no scientific training, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, ground his own lenses (he'd learned to use magnifiers to check thread counts in fabric) and made them better than anyone's in the world. Looking at a drop of pond water, he saw it swarming with tiny living things — “animalcules,” he called them — and later he saw bacteria, blood cells, and muscle fibers. When he wrote to the Royal Society in London, the scientists didn't believe him. A draper, seeing invisible animals? They sent people to check. He was right about all of it.

There's a NICU lesson hiding in there, and it's a hard one. Some of the tiny living things Leeuwenhoek saw are bacteria — single cells that can invade a body. A premature baby's defenses against them are weak, and an infection that spreads through the blood (sepsis) is one of the leading killers in a NICU, right alongside breathing problems. Which is why the most important machine in any NICU isn't a machine at all: it's the sink by the door, and the rule that everyone washes their hands, every time. Cells can save a life; cells can also take one. Biology doesn't pick sides.
