The Heel Prick

Every baby born in a hospital gets one: a tiny poke in the heel, a few drops of blood on a paper card, and a test for dozens of hidden typos in the instruction book — before any of them can do harm. It was invented by a man whose own family had been hurt by the one that came first.

🔗 From recipe to job: the chain

Last week: a gene is a recipe for one protein. This week, one more link. Many proteins are enzymes — proteins whose job is to do a chemical task, over and over, fast. An enzyme is a tiny worker with one skill: this one snips a molecule in half, that one glues two together, another one takes apart a sugar. Your body has thousands of kinds. The ones in your mitochondria burn sugar; the ones in your stomach digest dinner; the one in a newborn's liver clears bilirubin (slowly, at first — which is jaundice).

So the chain is: gene → protein (enzyme) → job. And here's what happens when there's a typo in the recipe. The enzyme comes out misshapen and can't do its job. Whatever it was supposed to take apart doesn't get taken apart. It piles up — yesterday's Calculus word — a little more every day, and in a baby, the piling-up happens inside a brain that is wiring itself at a million connections a second.

The clearest example has a name you'll hear in every NICU: PKU, short for phenylketonuria (fen-il-KEE-toe-NOOR-ee-uh). Proteins are built from twenty kinds of building block, and one of them is called phenylalanine. It's in milk, meat, eggs — nearly everything. A healthy body has an enzyme that breaks down the extra. A baby with PKU has a typo in that enzyme's gene, so the enzyme doesn't work, and phenylalanine piles up in the blood — and poisons the growing brain. A baby with PKU looks completely healthy at birth. By six months, the damage is starting. By a few years, it's severe and permanent.

And here is the part that makes PKU the most important disease you've never heard of: it's completely preventable. Feed the baby a special diet very low in phenylalanine, starting in the first weeks of life, and the brain develops normally. A German doctor named Horst Bickel proved that in 1953 with a toddler named Sheila. The only problem was that nobody could tell which newborn had PKU until it was too late.

🩸 Three drops on a card

In 1961, a microbiologist in Buffalo, New York named Robert Guthrie solved the “too late” problem. He studied bacteria for a living, and he knew a strain that would only grow if it was given plenty of phenylalanine. So he put a drop of blood on a disc of paper, set it on a dish of those bacteria, and waited. If the blood had normal phenylalanine, nothing much happened. If it had the high levels of PKU, the bacteria feasted, and a fat ring of growth appeared around the paper disc. A baby with PKU could be spotted days after birth — before any harm — from three drops of blood.

His second idea was just as important: the card. Blood dropped onto special filter paper, dried, could be mailed anywhere and tested weeks later. That meant a hospital in a small town could screen every baby by sending cards to one big laboratory. Massachusetts made the test required for every newborn in 1963; within a decade it was most of the world. The heel prick has been called the Guthrie test ever since.

Today's card works like this. At 24 to 48 hours of age — long enough that the baby has been feeding and any pile-up has started — a nurse warms the heel, pricks the side of it with a tiny lancet (the side has plenty of blood and no bone underneath), and squeezes drops onto a card printed with circles, filling each one. The card dries and goes to the state lab. In the NICU, the babies get the same card, sometimes twice, because TPN and blood transfusions can muddle the first result. And the poke is planned the way you learned in Psychology: a drop of sugar water, a parent's chest if possible, swaddled, quick.

Those are five of about thirty-five conditions on a modern American screening card — most of them exactly this pattern: a broken enzyme, something piling up, a treatment that works if you start early. Nearly four million babies a year are screened in the United States alone. And two more screens ride along that don't use blood at all: a hearing test (tiny earphones play clicks while a sensor listens for the brain's echo) and a heart test — a pulse oximeter on the right hand and a foot at 24 hours, looking for the oxygen mismatch that reveals a heart built with a missing pipe. 🤔 Why test everyone for something so rare?

PKU happens in about one baby in every twelve thousand. Hypothyroidism, about one in three thousand. Most of the conditions on the card are rarer still. So why prick every single baby? Because a screening test only makes sense when four things line up, and for these they do:

  1. The disease hides. The baby looks perfect. There is no way to spot it by looking.
  2. Waiting is a disaster. By the time the signs show, the harm is done.
  3. Early treatment works. A diet, a pill, a plan — and the child grows up well.
  4. The test is cheap and safe. A few drops, a card, a stamp.

When those four are true, testing twelve thousand babies to find one is a bargain, and every country that can afford it does. When one of them is false — no treatment exists, or the test would be a big operation — screening stops making sense, and doctors argue (carefully, with statistics) about where the line goes. Which is why the card has grown from one disease in 1963 to about thirty-five now, one hard-won argument at a time.

One more idea, and Statistics will make a whole lecture of it in Week 5: a screen is a net, not a verdict. To make sure it never misses a real case, the net is set wide, so it also catches some healthy babies. A “positive” heel prick means “test this baby again, properly, now” — and most of the time, the second test comes back normal. Families get a scary phone call and then relief. That's the price of a net that doesn't miss.

🔬 Home lab: a working enzyme, and a broken one

You need: a raw potato, a cooked (boiled or microwaved) piece of the same potato, hydrogen peroxide from the first-aid kit, two small cups, and a grown-up.

Put a slice of raw potato in one cup and a slice of cooked potato in the other. Pour a spoonful of hydrogen peroxide over each. The raw slice fizzes and foams: potato cells are full of an enzyme called catalase, whose job is to snip hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen gas, and the bubbles are the oxygen. The cooked slice does nothing. Heat warped the enzyme's shape, exactly the way a typo warps the PKU enzyme — same potato, same peroxide, but the worker is broken, so the job doesn't get done and the peroxide just sits there, piled up. That's PKU in a cup. The homework has a place to record it.

And the bilirubin check from Lesson 7 is a screen for an enzyme that's merely slow, not broken — which is why jaundice usually fixes itself and PKU never does.

👨‍👦 Real talk: the man who wouldn't sell his test

Robert Guthrie had a reason to care. His son John was born with an intellectual disability that no one could explain. Then his wife's niece was diagnosed with PKU — at fifteen months old, after the damage had begun. Guthrie was not a baby doctor; he was a bacteria scientist who had spent years on cancer research. He turned his lab toward one question: how do you find PKU in a baby who looks fine? The answer took him about a year. Then he did something unusual. He refused to let his test be sold for profit, fought the company that tried to price it high, and spent the rest of his life pushing states and countries to screen every baby, with the cards he'd designed sent to him from all over the world. He died in 1995. Roughly every baby born in a hospital since has had his test.

The honest part has three pieces. First, the net catches healthy babies too: a positive screen is frightening, and most turn out fine — that isn't a flaw, it's the design, but families deserve to be told so, kindly and fast. Second, some conditions on the card have no cure yet; the screen buys time and knowledge, not always a fix. Third — and Sociology will take this up tomorrow — a card is only as good as the country's ability to mail it, test it, and call the family back. In many parts of the world, no one is screened at all, and children still grow up with PKU that a diet could have prevented. Genetics finds the typo. Getting the diet to the baby is a different science.

One of the twenty protein building blocks. Piles up in PKU.
