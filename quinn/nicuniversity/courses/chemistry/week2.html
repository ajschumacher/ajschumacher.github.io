🍋 What an acid is

Lick a lemon. That sour punch is an acid — and every sour thing you've ever tasted (vinegar, oranges, sour candy, the fizz in soda) is sour for the same reason. An acid is a chemical that lets go of tiny loose pieces of hydrogen. Remember hydrogen, H, the smallest atom of all? An acid molecule drops off its hydrogens the way a bus drops off passengers, and those loose hydrogens go bumping into everything. They're what your tongue tastes as sour, and — in strong enough acids — what eats holes in metal.

A base is the opposite kind of chemical: it grabs loose hydrogens and takes them out of circulation. Bases feel slippery and taste bitter — soap, baking soda, and the stuff that unclogs drains are all bases. (Don't lick those.) And here is the neat trick: mix an acid with a base and they cancel each other out. The loose hydrogens from the acid get caught by the base, and what's left is usually just water and a salt. Chemists say the two have neutralized. You've seen it: baking soda (a base) plus vinegar (an acid) fizzes furiously. The fizz is carbon dioxide — CO₂, your old friend from last week — escaping as the two cancel.

📏 The pH ruler

“Sour” and “very sour” aren't good enough for science. In 1909 a Danish chemist named Søren Sørensen invented a number for it. He worked at the Carlsberg brewery in Copenhagen — beer-making is chemistry, and the brewers needed to know exactly how acidic their vats were — and he called his number pH. It runs from 0 to 14:

  • 7 is neutral — pure water. Neither acid nor base.
  • Below 7 is acid. The lower the number, the stronger the acid.
  • Above 7 is base. The higher, the stronger the base.

Now the part that surprises everyone. The pH ruler is not an ordinary ruler. Each step down is ten times more acidic. pH 5 is ten times as acidic as pH 6. pH 4 is ten times as acidic as pH 5 — which makes it a hundred times as acidic as pH 6. Go from pH 7 down to pH 1, six steps, and you've multiplied by ten six times: a million. That's why a scale from just 0 to 14 can cover everything from drain cleaner to stomach acid.

🔬 Home lab: make your own pH tester

You need: a few leaves of red cabbage, hot water, a jar, and small cups of things to test (lemon juice, vinegar, milk, water, baking-soda water, soapy water).

Chop the cabbage, soak it in hot water for ten minutes, and pour off the purple juice. That juice is an indicator — a dye that changes color with pH. Add a spoonful to each cup: acids turn it pink or red, bases turn it blue or green, neutral stays purple. Line the cups up from reddest to greenest and you've built Sørensen's ruler on your kitchen table. The homework has a place to record it. (Chemists have used plant dyes this way since the 1600s — the first one, litmus, comes from a lichen.)

🩸 Blood: the fussiest number in the body

Look back at the ruler. Blood sits at 7.35 to 7.45. That window is one-tenth of one step wide. Your stomach is happy at 2; your skin is fine at 5; but blood that drifts to 7.2 makes a NICU team move fast, and blood at 6.9 or 7.8 is usually fatal. Why so picky?

Because of proteins. Last week in Genetics you learned that proteins are the working machines of the body, built from gene recipes — hemoglobin, surfactant, the enzymes in your mitochondria that burn sugar. A protein works because of its exact folded shape, and loose hydrogens grab onto proteins and warp them. A little too much acid and the enzymes slow down, the heart muscle squeezes more weakly, and hemoglobin drops its oxygen too early. The whole chemistry set from Week 1 depends on staying at 7.4.

Where does the acid come from? Two places, and you already know both:

  • Carbon dioxide. When CO₂ dissolves in water it becomes carbonic acid (H₂CO₃ — count the atoms: 6). It's the same acid that makes soda water taste sharp. Every cell makes CO₂ every second, so the body is always making acid, and the lungs are always breathing it away. Stop breathing well and the acid piles up within minutes.
  • Starving cells. When cells can't get enough oxygen, they switch to an emergency way of burning sugar that makes lactic acid. You've felt it — the burn in your legs after a hard sprint. In a baby it means some part of the body isn't getting enough blood or oxygen.

And the defense? A sponge. Blood carries a base called bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) that soaks up loose hydrogens before they do damage. You have some in your kitchen: baking soda is sodium bicarbonate — which is exactly why it fizzes against vinegar. Two organs run the whole balancing act. The lungs control the CO₂ side, and they work in minutes: breathe faster, blow off more acid. The kidneys control the bicarbonate side, and they work over hours and days.

🧪 The blood gas: three drops, three clues

Now you can read the test. A blood gas is a few drops of blood — from a warmed heel, or painlessly from an umbilical line like the ones in NICU Tutor Lesson 4 — fed into a machine that answers in about a minute. It reports several numbers; the three that matter most today are the pH, the amount of CO₂, and the amount of bicarbonate. The doctor reads them like a detective reads clues:

ClueNormal (about)Too low means…Too high means…
pH7.35–7.45too much acid (acidosis)too little acid (alkalosis)
CO₂35–45 mmHgbreathing too much — blowing off too muchbreathing too little — CO₂ piling up
Bicarbonate22–26the sponge is being used up by acid from the cellsthe kidneys are holding extra sponge

Put the clues together and the gas tells a story:

  • pH low and CO₂ high. The acid is carbonic acid, and it's piling up because the lungs aren't clearing it. This is a breathing problem — a baby having apnea spells, or one whose CPAP isn't enough. The fix is more breathing help: turn up the CPAP, add a ventilator breath, give caffeine.
  • pH low but CO₂ normal, bicarbonate low. The lungs are doing their job, so the acid is coming from somewhere else — starving cells. Something is stopping oxygen from reaching part of the body: an infection, low blood pressure, a sick gut. The fix isn't the breathing machine; it's finding and treating the cause.
  • pH high and CO₂ low. The machine is breathing too much for the baby, blowing off too much CO₂. That's dangerous too: low CO₂ squeezes the blood vessels in a preemie's brain. Turn the ventilator down.

Notice what the last one means. A blood gas isn't just about finding too little breathing; it's how the team keeps from doing too much. Remember Physics last week — “the smallest pressure that works”? The blood gas is how they know what works. Modern NICUs will even let a baby's CO₂ run a little high, with a pH down near 7.25, rather than crank up a ventilator that could injure the lungs. The number they're protecting is 7.4; the thing they're protecting is a baby.

⏪ Flashback: “A's and B's”

In NICU Tutor Lesson 3 you met apnea — a preemie simply forgetting to breathe. Now you can say what happens in the blood during those twenty seconds: CO₂ keeps arriving from every cell, nothing carries it out, carbonic acid climbs, pH slides. That's why a bad spell gets a blood gas afterward, and why caffeine — which keeps the breathing center awake — is really an acid-control medicine in disguise.

🍺 Real talk: the brewery, the soda water, and the drops

Sørensen's ruler came out of a beer factory because brewing is a fight over acid: too much and the beer spoils, too little and the yeast sulks. He needed a number, so he invented one, and within a few decades every hospital in the world was using it. And the fizzy water Joseph Priestley invented in 1767 — the man from last week who discovered oxygen — is carbon dioxide dissolved in water: carbonic acid, the very same acid a baby's lungs must blow away all day. Chemistry doesn't care whether it's in a bottle, a brewery, or a bloodstream. The rules are the rules.

Here's the honest part. Every blood gas costs a poke and a few drops, and a 1-kilogram preemie has only about 80 milliliters of blood in its whole body — a third of a cup. This afternoon, Biology will show you what that blood is made of, and why NICU teams count every drop they take. Good units bundle their blood tests together, use the umbilical line while it's in, and ask before every test: will this number change what we do? If the answer is no, the baby keeps the drops.
