The Chemistry of a Breath

Take a breath. In that one second, about a sextillion tiny particles poured into your lungs, a few of them were traded for others, and the rest went right back out. Chemistry is the science of those particles — and a NICU is, among other things, a very careful chemistry set.

⚛️ Everything is made of atoms

Here is the biggest idea in all of chemistry, and it fits in one sentence: everything you can touch is made of atoms. Your desk, the air, a baby's fingernail, the ocean, you. Atoms are almost unimaginably small — a single drop of water contains more atoms than there are stars in our entire galaxy. You can't see one with any ordinary microscope. And yet everything is built from them, the way every LEGO castle is built from bricks.

How many kinds of brick are there? About 118 — each kind is called an element. Gold is an element (all its atoms are gold atoms). So are oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, iron, and calcium. Here's the astonishing part: living things are built mostly from just four elements — carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen — with a sprinkle of a dozen others. Four kinds of brick, and they build every baby ever born.

Chemists give each element a short symbol so they don't have to keep writing the whole name: H for hydrogen, O for oxygen, C for carbon, N for nitrogen. (Some symbols look odd because they come from Latin — iron is Fe, from ferrum. That's why doctors say a baby is “iron-deficient” and write “Fe.”)

Write this down · big idea 1

Everything is made of atoms. There are about 118 kinds (elements); living things use mostly C, H, O, and N.

🤝 Molecules: atoms holding hands

Atoms almost never travel alone. They grab onto each other in specific groups called molecules, and a molecule can behave completely differently from the atoms inside it. Two hydrogens plus one oxygen makes water — a liquid you can drink, made from two gases you can't.

Chemists write a molecule's recipe as a chemical formula. The rule is simple: write each element's symbol, and if there's more than one of that atom, write how many as a little low number — a subscript — right after it. No number means one.

Now a bigger one. Sugar — the kind your cells burn for energy — is glucose: C₆H₁₂O₆. Read the recipe: 6 carbons, 12 hydrogens, 6 oxygens. Total atoms? 6 + 12 + 6 = 24. One sugar molecule is a little sculpture of 24 atoms, and NICU babies get it dripped straight into their veins when they're too small to eat.

🧪 Try it yourself: count the atoms

Cover the answers and count: How many atoms in NH₃ (ammonia)? In C₂H₆O (rubbing alcohol — the smell of every hospital)? In CaCO₃ (chalk, and also seashells)? Answers: 4, 9, 5. Trick to check yourself: add up every plain letter as 1 and every subscript as its number.

Write this down · big idea 2

A formula is a molecule's recipe. The little number (subscript) counts the atom just before it. No number = 1.

🌬️ What's actually in the air

People say “we breathe oxygen,” but that's only a fifth true. Air is a mixture of gases, and chemists describe mixtures with percent — out of every 100 particles, how many are which kind:

NitrogenN₂about 78%about 78% (unchanged)
OxygenO₂about 21%about 16% — some was used

Carbon dioxideCO₂about 0.04% (a trace)about 4% — a hundred times more!

So most of every breath is nitrogen, and your body does nothing with it — in and right back out, like a passenger who never gets off the bus. The oxygen is the cargo that matters: your body keeps about a quarter of what comes in. And look at the last two rows: you breathe out a hundred times more carbon dioxide than you breathe in, plus a cloud of water. Your body is a little factory that turns O₂ into CO₂ and H₂O — and Biology 101 will show you exactly where, this afternoon.

⏪ Flashback: “21%”

Remember the pulse oximeter lesson, where NICU teams turn a baby's oxygen up and down? The machine that mixes it is called a blender, and its dial runs from 21% to 100%. Now you know why it starts at 21: that's plain room air — the blender adds extra oxygen from there. A baby “on 30%” is breathing air with a little extra; “on 100%” is breathing pure oxygen. And you remember from NICU Tutor Lesson 2 why the team never leaves it at 100% longer than it must.

🫁 The trade inside a breath

Air flows down into your lungs and fills the alveoli — those hundreds of millions of microscopic balloons from NICU Tutor Lesson 5. Their walls are so thin that gas molecules can slip straight through them. And here a trade happens, called gas exchange:

  • Oxygen goes in: O₂ molecules cross from the air into the blood, where hemoglobin grabs them and carries them off to every cell in the body.
  • Carbon dioxide comes out: CO₂ molecules, delivered by the blood from all those cells, cross the other way into the air — and you breathe them away.

Why must the CO₂ leave? Because carbon dioxide dissolved in blood makes an acid. A little is normal and even useful — your body uses it as a signal to breathe. But if breathing fails and CO₂ piles up, the blood turns too acidic, and the body's chemistry starts going wrong — heart, brain, everything. This is exactly why the NICU team watches a baby's breathing so closely, and why they run a test called a blood gas that measures the oxygen, the CO₂, and the acid in a few drops of blood. You'll meet acids and bases properly next Monday.

🐭 Real talk: the mouse in the jar

In 1774, an English minister and amateur chemist named Joseph Priestley focused sunlight through a magnifying glass onto a reddish powder (mercury calx) and collected the gas that fizzed off. He put a candle in it: the flame blazed brilliantly. He put a mouse in a sealed jar of it: the mouse lived about twice as long as a mouse in a jar of ordinary air. He breathed some himself and reported that his chest felt “peculiarly light and easy.” He had discovered oxygen — the one-fifth of air that matters — though he never quite understood what he'd found. A French chemist, Antoine Lavoisier, worked out that this gas was a true element and named it oxygène.

Here's the sobering link to the NICU. Priestley's discovery is why we can give babies extra oxygen at all — and for 170 years afterward, doctors assumed more must always be better. It took until the 1950s, and thousands of babies blinded, to learn that oxygen is a medicine with a dose. Chemistry finds the molecule; medicine has to learn how much. (Priestley, for what it's worth, also invented fizzy water. He was having a very good decade.)

📖 Words to know
Atom
The tiniest building block of ordinary matter.
Element
One kind of atom — oxygen, carbon, gold. About 118 exist.
Molecule
Two or more atoms joined together — O₂, CO₂, H₂O.
Chemical formula / subscript
The recipe for a molecule; the little number counts the atom before it.
Percent
Out of a hundred. Air is 21% oxygen: 21 particles in every 100.
Gas exchange
The oxygen-in, carbon-dioxide-out trade across the alveoli.
Acid
A sour, chemically pushy substance. Dissolved CO₂ makes one — and blood has to stay very nearly not-acidic.
