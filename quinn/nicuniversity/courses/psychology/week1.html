🔎 A science of minds that can't talk

Psychology is the science of the mind and behavior — what people think, feel, and do. Most psychologists can simply ask their subjects questions. Baby psychologists can't. So they invented a rule that turns out to be one of the smartest ideas in all of science: if you can't ask, measure what they do.

Babies do three things that give their minds away:

  • They look. Babies stare longer at things that are new or interesting and get bored (look away) from things they've seen before. In the 1960s, psychologist Robert Fantz put pictures in front of infants and simply timed their gaze — and discovered babies prefer patterns to plain colors, and faces to almost everything. Looking time became baby science's most powerful tool.
  • They suck. Babies suck harder or in different rhythms when something interests them. Hook a pacifier to a recorder, and a baby can “choose” between two sounds with its mouth.
  • Their bodies react. Heart rate, breathing, and stress hormones change when a baby is startled, soothed, or in pain — all things a NICU monitor can measure.

No words needed. A baby's behavior is its answer.

👂 What a newborn already knows

Using those tools, here is what scientists have learned a baby brings into the world:

Hearing starts in the womb. A baby's ears switch on around 24 to 25 weeks — the age of the earliest preemies. For the last three months before birth, a baby hears its mother's heartbeat, her voice (muffled, like through a wall), and the world outside. In 1980, psychologists Anthony DeCasper and William Fifer gave newborns a pacifier wired to a tape player: sucking one way played their mother's voice, sucking another way played a stranger's. Babies less than three days old sucked to hear mom. In a famous follow-up, mothers read The Cat in the Hat aloud twice a day for the last six weeks of pregnancy. After birth, the babies sucked to hear that story over a different one. They remembered it from inside the womb.

Faces come first. Within hours of birth, babies turn to follow a face-like pattern (two dots over a line) more than the same shapes scrambled. Nobody taught them. And newborn eyes focus best at about 20 to 30 centimeters — which happens to be exactly how far away a parent's face is during feeding. Babies are built to see the person holding them, and not much else.

Smell and taste are ready. Within days, a newborn turns toward a cloth that smells like its own mother and away from a stranger's. Newborns prefer sweet tastes from birth — a fact NICUs use, as you'll see.

Now think about a preemie born at 26 weeks. Its ears have just switched on. Everything it would have heard muffled through its mother's body, it now hears raw: alarms, voices, the hiss of CPAP, doors. That's why modern NICUs measure noise levels, and why parents are asked to talk, read, and sing to their babies. Studies of preemies who heard recordings of their mother's voice in the isolette found their hearing brain areas developed more. The Cat in the Hat study wasn't just cute. It told NICUs what to do.

🧠 A brain wiring itself

Inside a newborn's head are about 86 billion brain cells, called neurons. They're mostly all there at birth — but they're barely connected. In the first years of life, neurons link up at a staggering pace: more than a million new connections every second. And here's the part that makes psychology matter for the NICU: the connections that form depend on experience. Every voice, face, touch, and cuddle — and every alarm, bright light, and painful poke — is shaping which wires get built.

A full-term baby does the last three months of that wiring — the third trimester — in the dark, warm, muffled quiet of the womb. A preemie does it in a NICU. In the 1980s, a Harvard psychologist named Heidelise Als sat for hours beside isolettes doing what psychologists do: watching behavior. She showed that preemies were communicating the whole time — a splayed hand, a grimace, a color change, a startle meant “too much.” Curling up, a steady color, a relaxed face meant “okay.” She trained nurses to read those signals and to change the NICU around them: dim the lights, hush the alarms, group the pokes so the baby could sleep, tuck babies into nests, bring parents close. That approach is called developmental care, and it's why NICUs today look the way they do — softer, darker, quieter than they did forty years ago.

Seen this way, kangaroo care is brain care. A parent's chest gives a preemie the closest thing to the womb the NICU can offer: warmth, a heartbeat, a familiar voice, and deep, protected sleep — exactly the conditions a wiring brain wants.

🧪 Try it yourself: the voice test

Stand outside a room with two or three family members inside. Have them take turns saying the same sentence in a normal voice while you try to name who's speaking. You'll get nearly every one right — your brain learned those voices the same way a newborn learns its mother's: by hearing them again and again. Bonus: try it with your eyes closed and notice your head turn toward the sound. That head-turn is one of the behaviors baby scientists measure.

💔 Real talk: “babies don't feel pain”

Here is a hard truth about medicine's past. Until the 1980s, many doctors believed newborns didn't really feel pain — their brains were “too immature,” the thinking went, and they wouldn't remember anyway. So babies had blood drawn, tubes placed, and even surgery with little or no pain medicine. It took a psychologist's kind of thinking to end it: measure, don't assume. In 1987, a young doctor named K.J.S. Anand measured the stress hormones and heart rates of newborns during operations and found huge stress responses — and that giving proper anesthesia reduced them and helped the babies recover. The evidence was so clear that practice changed within a few years.

Today NICUs treat newborn pain as real because it is. Nurses score a baby's pain the way Apgar scored a newborn (face, cry, body, breathing — numbers again). Babies get a drop of sugar water before a heel prick, because sweetness calms them. Parents are invited to hold their baby skin-to-skin during pokes, because it measurably lowers the stress response. And everyone tries to poke less. The lesson isn't that the old doctors were cruel — most weren't. It's that a kind person with a wrong belief can still do harm, and the cure is measurement. Remember that when you're the one in charge.

📖 Words to know
Psychology
The science of mind and behavior.
Behavior
What someone does — a baby's only way of answering.
Looking time
How long a baby stares at something; longer means newer or more interesting.
Neurons
Brain cells that connect to each other — over a million connections a second in early life.
Third trimester
The last three months of pregnancy, when the brain and lungs do their finishing work.
Developmental care
Running the NICU around the baby's cues: dim, quiet, protected sleep, parents close.
