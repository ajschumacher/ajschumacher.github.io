📖 Glossary: Talk Like a Doctor

Real medical words, plainly explained. Doctor-language is mostly Latin and Greek LEGO bricks — learn the bricks and you can decode words you've never seen.

🧱 The LEGO bricks
  • hypo- = too little  ·  hyper- = too much
  • brady- = slow  ·  tachy- = fast
  • a- / an- = without  ·  -pnea = breathing  ·  -cardia = heart
  • neo- = new  ·  natal = birth  ·  -ologist = expert in
  • -itis = inflammation  ·  -pathy = disease  ·  -gram = picture or recording

So a neonatologist is a new-birth-expert, bradycardia is slow-heart, apnea is without-breathing, and tachypnea — a word you haven't even studied — is… fast breathing. See? Decoded.

The people

Neonatologist
A doctor specializing in newborns — especially sick and premature ones. First pediatrics training, then extra years of NICU training.
NICU nurse
The person at the bedside hour after hour. Nurses catch most problems first and do most of the hands-on care.
Respiratory therapist
The breathing-machine specialist: runs ventilators and CPAP and helps at every delivery of a fragile baby.
Attending / fellow / resident
The doctor ranks: the attending leads, the fellow is training to be a neonatologist, the resident is earlier in training. They walk rounds together every day.

The babies

Neonate
A baby in the first month of life.
Gestational age
How many weeks the baby grew in the womb before birth. Full-term is about 40 weeks; “premature” means born before 37.
Preemie / micro-preemie
A baby born early; “micro-preemie” usually means born before ~26 weeks or under ~800 grams.
LBW / VLBW / ELBW
Low (under 2500 g), very low (under 1500 g), and extremely low (under 1000 g) birth weight.
Corrected age
Age counted from the due date instead of the birthday. A 3-month-old born 2 months early has a corrected age of 1 month — used for judging growth and milestones.
Viability
The earliest a baby can possibly survive outside the womb — currently around 22–24 weeks, the very edge of what medicine can support.

Machines & tools

Isolette (incubator)
The clear warm-and-humid bed with portholes. Lesson 1.
Pulse oximeter
The glowing foot band measuring oxygen saturation with light. Lesson 2.
Cardiorespiratory monitor
The screen tracking every heartbeat and breath via chest leads. Lesson 3.
Arterial line / umbilical catheter
Thin tubes in blood vessels (including the belly-button vessels!) for live blood pressure and painless blood samples. Lesson 4.
CPAP
Steady air pressure through nose prongs that keeps lungs partly inflated. Lesson 5.
Ventilator
The machine that delivers measured breaths through a windpipe tube. Lesson 5.
NG tube & syringe pump
Nose-to-stomach milk delivery, slow and precise. Lesson 6.
PICC line
A long ultra-thin IV threaded to near the heart, for TPN and medicines. Lesson 6.
Bili lights
Blue phototherapy lights that dismantle bilirubin through the skin. Lesson 7.
Ultrasound / X-ray / echo
The see-inside tools: sound-pictures, shadow-pictures, and heart-movies. Lesson 8.

Body science

Thermoregulation
Keeping body temperature steady. Preemies can't; isolettes do it for them.
Brown fat
Heat-generating fat that newborns use instead of shivering.
Hemoglobin
The oxygen-carrying protein that makes blood red — brighter red when loaded.
Saturation (SpO₂)
The percentage of hemoglobin carrying oxygen right now.
Alveoli
The lungs' hundreds of millions of microscopic air balloons.
Surfactant
The soap-like coating that stops wet alveoli from sticking shut. Missing in preemies; givable as medicine.
Bilirubin
Yellow leftover from recycling old red blood cells; causes jaundice when it piles up.
Fontanelle
The soft spot — the skull's unclosed window, used for brain ultrasounds.
Ductus arteriosus
The womb-era vessel detouring blood past the unused lungs; supposed to close after birth.
Perfusion
Blood actually reaching the tissues and making its deliveries.

Conditions

Apnea of prematurity
Breathing pauses because the brain's breathing center is immature. Treated with touch, caffeine, and CPAP.
Bradycardia
Too-slow heart rate — apnea's frequent partner (“A's and B's”).
RDS
Respiratory distress syndrome: the sticky-lung disease from missing surfactant.
BPD (bronchopulmonary dysplasia)
Lung scarring and injury some preemies develop after long breathing support and oxygen — a big reason the NICU weans support as fast as safely possible.
ROP (retinopathy of prematurity)
Abnormal eye-vessel growth in preemies, worsened by too much oxygen; can cause blindness; watched for with eye exams.
Jaundice / hyperbilirubinemia
Yellowing from bilirubin buildup. Usually mild; treated with light when high.
Kernicterus
Permanent brain damage from extreme untreated bilirubin. Preventable with checks and treatment.
NEC (necrotizing enterocolitis)
The feared gut disease of preemies. Breast milk and careful feeding lower the risk.
IVH (intraventricular hemorrhage)
Brain bleed of very premature babies, graded 1–4, screened for with head ultrasounds.
PDA (patent ductus arteriosus)
The womb-detour vessel that failed to close.
Sepsis
Infection spreading through the bloodstream. Preemies' immune systems are weak, so NICUs fight germs with obsessive handwashing — the single best germ-defense ever invented.
